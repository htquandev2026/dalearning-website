Vietnamese pronouns: why you don’t say “tôi” to grandma

Textbooks teach “tôi” for “I” and “bạn” for “you”. They are correct, and you will almost never use them with family. Vietnamese mostly uses family words as pronouns: you call the other person by their place in the family, and you call yourself by yours.

Why not “tôi”?

“Tôi” is neutral and a little distant. It is right in a shop, at work, in writing, or with an adult you do not know well. Said to your grandmother it sounds as if you are keeping her at arm’s length, like calling your mother “madam”. Families notice it at once, and switching to “con” or “cháu” is the fastest way to sound like part of the family.

Who calls whom what

  • Grandparents: call them ông / bà, yourself cháu (many families also use con).
  • Parents, and your partner’s parents: bố mẹ or ba má; yourself con.
  • Aunts and uncles: bác, chú, cô, cậu, dì; yourself cháu. Who is which: Vietnamese family terms.
  • Older brother or sister, and older cousins: anh / chị; yourself em.
  • Younger brother or sister: em; yourself anh (male) or chị (female).
  • Partner: usually anh for the man or older partner, em for the woman or younger one. See I love you in Vietnamese.
  • Strangers: guess the family role by age. A bit older: anh / chị. Your parents’ age: cô / chú. Elderly: bác, or ông / bà. Younger: em.
  • Friends your own age: mình / bạn; in the North also tớ / cậu.

The other person answers the same way. Grandma calls herself “bà” and calls you “cháu” or “con”: “Bà thương cháu” is her “I love you”.

Small rules that help

  • We, to elders: “chúng con” or “chúng cháu”. Your partner and you, casually: “tụi con” in the South.
  • He or she: add “ấy”: “bà ấy”, “anh ấy”, “chị ấy”.
  • Not sure? Ask: “Con gọi bác ấy là gì ạ?” (What should I call him or her?)
  • When in doubt, go one step more respectful. Calling someone chị when she expected em is flattering; the other way round is not.
